Shi Meiyu (Mary Stone)

Words another nurse could use

Read the narration

A nurse may understand a lesson in training and still need help when a patient is in front of her. Shi Meiyu wanted nurses in China to have words they could use. Known in English as Mary Stone, she translated medical textbooks and training manuals into Chinese.

She'd grown up in a Chinese Christian family and studied medicine in Michigan. Then she returned to China, where caring for patients and teaching nurses became part of her daily work. A clearly explained lesson could help a nurse care for someone Mary would never meet.

The work depended on others, too. Her sister Phoebe, also a doctor, took responsibility for the hospital while Mary continued her studies. Later Mary helped establish the Bethel Mission. War eventually displaced the communities and institutions she helped build.

Romans describes people with different gifts who belong to one body in Christ. God's grace gives us something to offer and people whose help we need.

Perhaps someone could use what you've learned. Take time to explain it in words they can understand. When we teach with patience, we help another person care for a neighbor. That shared work is one way we serve the Lord together.

Knowledge that could serve another patient

Shi Meiyu, known in English as Mary Stone, studied medicine at the University of Michigan with Kang Cheng and returned to China. Medical care and nursing education developed together in her work. Translating training materials into Chinese helped make knowledge usable by people who would care for patients themselves.

Her service depended on colleagues and family. Her sister Phoebe took responsibility for the hospital during further study, and Shi later helped establish the Bethel Mission with Jennie Hughes. War disrupted the communities and institutions in which they worked. Remembering that shared effort gives a fuller account than attributing every hospital or training outcome to one person.

The distinction between teaching and direct care can disappear in a useful lesson: what one person learns may become part of another neighbor’s treatment. Romans 12 gives us a way to value these different gifts together.
