Nerses and Armenian communities of care

A place at the shared table

Read the narration

Nerses, a church leader in fourth-century Armenia, shared his table with people who were often pushed aside.

The Armenian history attributed to Pawstos describes him dividing food with his own hands and tending to wounded people. Blind, deaf and disabled guests sat among his companions.

Care also became a shared responsibility. Bishops gathered at Ashtishat to arrange provision for people in need. There were houses for the sick and destitute, and support for widows and orphans.

Those arrangements expected recipients to remain in designated places instead of going out to beg. Shelter came with restrictions on the people receiving it.

Romans calls believers to contribute to one another's needs and pursue hospitality. We do this because God has already shown us mercy in Christ. Our generosity can't purchase his love.

A church needs organized provision and people willing to keep promises. It also needs the kindness of shared company. As we offer help, we can listen to the person receiving it and respect their choices.

Who could you welcome to a meal or a conversation? Ask what would help, and listen to the answer. Give someone room to be known as a person whose company you value, with a place in your shared life.

A table and organized care in Armenia

P’awstos’s History of the Armenians remembers fourth-century bishop Nerses sharing food with poor and disabled people and tending wounds himself. Book IV, chapter 4 also describes organized provision for sick people, widows and orphans.

This is a later literary account of the fourth-century church, not a contemporary hospital ledger. It brings together charitable institutions, episcopal ideals and religious expectations. The arrangements it describes could provide shelter while restricting recipients’ freedom to leave designated places. Compassion must attend to dignity and agency as well as material provision.

The table gives us a personal question alongside the institutional one: who is welcome to share ordinary life with us? Romans 12’s instructions about generosity and hospitality invite both practical organization and relationships in which people can belong.

Pause & consider

Where could practical help also make room for friendship and another person’s agency?
